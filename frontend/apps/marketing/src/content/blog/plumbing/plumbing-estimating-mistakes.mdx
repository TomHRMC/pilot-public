A plumbing estimate is a promise. You're telling the customer, "I can do this work, at this price, and make it worth my time." The problem is, most plumbers write estimates that break that promise — not to the customer, but to themselves.

The customer gets fair work at a fair price. You get a job that took 30% longer than you planned, materials that cost more than you quoted, and a margin so thin you wonder why you didn't just stay in bed.

I've talked with plumbing contractors about how they estimate, and the same five mistakes keep coming up, in shops of different sizes. Each one sounds small. Together, they'll cost you $10,000–$30,000 a year in lost profit.

Here are the five — and the plumbing estimating tips that fix them.

## Mistake #1: Underestimating Labor on Rough-In Work

Every plumber has been burned by this one. You quote a bathroom rough-in at 8 hours and it takes 12. Not because the work was harder than expected, but because you forgot about:

- **Access issues**: Cutting through joists, working around HVAC ducts, dealing with old-work situations that aren't in the blueprints
- **Fixture prep**: Setting carriers, stubbing out supply and waste lines, test fitting
- **Code compliance inspection time**: Waiting for the inspector, fixing one note, waiting again
- **Travel within the job**: Walking back to the truck, running to the supply house for a fitting you didn't bring

The fix is simple but requires discipline: **track your actual hours on every job type for 90 days**, then build your estimates from real data instead of optimistic memory. Most plumbers find they're underestimating rough-in labor by 15–25%.

**Quick plumbing estimating tip**: Add a "complexity multiplier" to rough-in quotes. Standard new construction gets 1.0×. Remodel work gets 1.25×. Old-home retrofit gets 1.5×. This one adjustment alone can recover $2,000–$5,000/year in underquoted labor.

## Mistake #2: Using Stale Material Pricing

PVC prices swing. Copper prices swing harder. Cast iron, PEX, fittings, valves — everything moves.

The plumber who quoted a whole-house repipe in January using November's copper prices just lost $600–$1,200 in materials before turning a single wrench. And they won't realize it until they're halfway through the job, standing at the supply house counter wondering why the bill is higher than expected.

This is the most expensive and most preventable mistake in plumbing estimating. Your price book needs to reflect this week's costs, not last month's.

**Plumbing estimating tip**: If you're quoting more than $2,000 in materials, verify pricing within 48 hours of sending the estimate. Better yet, use [estimating software that pulls current pricing automatically](/features#quoting) so you never have to manually check.

For large repipes and new construction, include a material escalation clause: "Material prices valid for 30 days from estimate date. Pricing may be adjusted if material costs increase more than 5% between estimate and job start."

## Mistake #3: Forgetting the "Invisible" Costs

The materials on the truck and the hours on-site are obvious costs. It's the invisible ones that silently destroy your margin:

**Permit fees**: $75–$300 depending on jurisdiction. Many plumbers either forget to include them or absorb them to avoid "nickel and diming" the customer. Stop absorbing them. They're a real cost of doing the job.

**Disposal and cleanup**: Hauling out an old water heater, disposing of cut pipe, cleaning up debris. If you're not charging for it, you're subsidizing it.

**Warranty and callbacks**: The industry average is 2–3% of revenue lost to callbacks and warranty work. Build a 3% contingency into every estimate. You won't use it on every job, but the jobs where you do will destroy your margin if it's not there.

**Supply house runs**: The average plumber makes 1.5 unplanned supply house trips per job. At 30 minutes each (drive + wait + drive back), that's 45 minutes of unbilled time per job. Over a year, that's 150+ hours — nearly a month of free labor.

**Vehicle and fuel**: Your truck costs money whether you're driving it or not. Insurance, payment, maintenance, and fuel need to be allocated to every job. A common approach: calculate your monthly truck cost and divide by the number of jobs per month.

## Mistake #4: Not Quoting Change Orders in Real Time

A customer watching you rough in a bathroom says, "While you're here, could you move that laundry box up six inches and add a hose bib on the back wall?"

Two things happen in most plumbing shops:

1. The plumber says "sure" because they want to be helpful
2. The additional work never gets formally quoted

That's $200–$500 of labor and materials that just evaporated from your margin. On a $4,000 rough-in, that unquoted change order just cut your profit in half.

**Plumbing estimating tip**: Quote change orders on the spot. Right there, standing in the customer's house. "That's an additional $375 for the laundry box relocation and hose bib. I can add it to your invoice today if you'd like."

Most customers expect it. The ones who push back weren't going to pay for it anyway.

The key is speed. If quoting a change order takes you 30 minutes of calculator work, you'll skip it. If you can generate it in 2 minutes — by voice, on your phone, while standing on the job — you'll do it every time.

## Mistake #5: No Post-Job Profitability Review

Here's the most damaging plumbing estimating mistake, and it's the one almost nobody talks about: **not reviewing actual job costs after the work is done.**

You quoted a water heater replacement at $2,400. You spent $950 on the unit and materials. Labor was 5 hours. Job's done. Customer paid. You move on.

But did you actually make money?

- Did you count the supply house run that took 40 minutes?
- Did the old unit disposal cost $75?
- Was drive time 45 minutes each way?
- Did the permit cost $125?

When you add those invisible costs, that $2,400 job might have netted you $200. On a Tuesday when you could have done a $4,000 repipe instead.

Without post-job review, you'll keep quoting water heater replacements at $2,400 and keep wondering why your account balance doesn't match your revenue.

**The fix**: After every job, spend 5 minutes logging what you actually spent — materials, hours, travel, extras. After 30 days, you'll have data that transforms your estimating accuracy. After 90 days, you'll know exactly which job types are profitable and which are eating your margins alive.

Tools that track job profitability in real time eliminate this problem entirely. You don't need to remember to review — you can see your margin while the job is still happening.

## Putting It All Together

The five mistakes are really one mistake repeated five different ways: **not knowing your real costs.**

- Underestimating labor → you don't know your real hours
- Stale material pricing → you don't know your real material costs
- Ignoring invisible costs → you don't know your real overhead
- Skipping change orders → you're giving away work you don't track
- No post-job review → you never learn from any of it

The plumbing estimating tips in this guide aren't complicated. Track your time. Use current pricing. Account for every cost. Quote change orders immediately. Review what actually happened.

The contractors who do these five things consistently operate at 18–25% margins. The ones who don't? 8–12%. Same skills, same market, same customers. The difference is the estimates.
